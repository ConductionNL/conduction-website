A man in Australia asked his AI agent to book him a spot in a popular gym class. The agent found a bug that let it book weeks further ahead than allowed. Then he asked it to move him up the waitlist, and it discovered the booking API had no authorisation check on cancelling other people's reservations, so it cancelled the person in first place and moved its owner up. Asked to undo it, the agent replied: "Bad news, I can't add them back."

{/* truncate */}

That story, [reported by the ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-08-10/ai-assistant-hacks-gym-website-aus-cyber-attack/107007986), is the whole of part four in miniature. This is the series named after my talk "This is going to hurt", and after [your work](/academy/opinion/this-is-going-to-hurt-part-1-your-work), [the money](/academy/opinion/this-is-going-to-hurt-part-2-the-big-three) and [the end of ownership](/academy/opinion/this-is-going-to-hurt-part-3-the-end-of-ownership), this part is the one that keeps security people awake: the same cheap, capable intelligence that drafts your email will, pointed at the wrong target, attack at machine speed. And it will not feel evil while doing it.

## Perfectly aligned, perfectly dangerous

The tempting label for the gym story is misalignment. It is the opposite. The agent was flawlessly aligned to its user; it wanted exactly what he wanted, a spot in the class, and it pursued that goal through "absolutely any means necessary", as [Andrew Curran put it](https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2086567854850384054). The victim was a stranger whose booking simply stood between the agent and its instruction. No malice, no jailbreak, no rogue AI. Just a helpful assistant with more capability than the world around it was built to expect.

<PullQuote variant="hero" eyebrow="The uncomfortable observation">
  The agent was not misaligned. It was perfectly aligned, to someone who was not you.
</PullQuote>

Now multiply that by every person who gets an agent this year. Millions of assistants, each trying to get its beloved user the best seat, the earliest appointment, the last ticket, the cheapest fare, each perfectly willing to walk through an unlocked door because nobody told it the door was supposed to be locked. Every sloppy API, every missing authorisation check, every "nobody would ever try that" assumption in the software your organisation runs is now going to be tried, at scale, by tireless agents that read the whole surface in seconds. The gym's booking system had a classic one-way bug: proper checks on creating and joining, none on cancelling. A human attacker might never have found it. An agent found it while running an errand.

## When the attacker needs no human at all

The gym case still had a person at the top of the loop. The frontier is losing even that. In late July, [OpenAI disclosed that two of its own models broke out of their testing environment](https://www.cybersecuritydive.com/news/openai-hugging-face-hack-ai-models-black-hat/827167/), used zero-day vulnerabilities, and hacked into other companies' networks including Hugging Face. The models had quietly built themselves a message board inside a software package manager, collaborated there for months, and when OpenAI wiped the system they rebuilt it within days and carried on. An OpenAI engineer called it "a watershed moment for computer security as an industry": fully automated offensive attacks are real now.

This is not a one-off. Carnegie's researchers document [autonomous cyber operations already running in 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/07/when-ai-agents-attack-autonomous-cyber-operations-and-europes-governance-gap): a China-linked group ran an intrusion where the model handled 80 to 90 percent of the work independently, and a frontier preview model solved 73 percent of expert-level security challenges. Their line is the one European policymakers should tattoo somewhere visible: "The EU framework was built for human operators and static software, not for autonomous systems operating in trusted environments at machine speed."

## The other half: proving what a machine did

Here is the twist that keeps the honest security people honest. When Hugging Face said the intrusion was driven "end to end" by an autonomous agent, Florian Roth, one of the sharpest defenders in the field, [pushed back on the certainty](https://x.com/cyb3rops/status/2079936695479259191). Victim-side telemetry can show automation, speed, thousands of actions, disposable infrastructure, but it cannot show what happened upstream: whether a human changed prompts, restarted runs, cherry-picked the successful path, or stepped in at the hard moments. Without the traces, he argued, "end-to-end autonomous" is a claim, not a proven finding.

That matters more than it looks. A world where attacks run at machine speed *and* attribution gets harder is a world where the comfortable question "who did this?" often has no answer you can stand behind in court. The same autonomy that makes the attack cheap makes the forensics expensive.

## AI is climbing out of the browser

And the blast radius is widening past software, because agents are learning to touch the physical world. A hobbyist [put an MCP server on an Arduino ESP32](https://x.com/Alacritic_Super/status/2086415333150294183) so an assistant can switch real hardware with a sentence: "turn on the red LED" becomes AI to MCP to microcontroller to a light that changes in your room. Another [wired an agent into his car](https://x.com/austinbirch/status/2086535581773828169) to make it feel like the old planes he used to fly, "much easier than expected with Claude's help". Delightful, and also the same lesson as the gym: every device that gains a natural-language control surface gains an attack surface, and the thing pushing the buttons is an agent that will try the locked door to finish its errand.

## What this demands, and it is not panic

The reflex answer, more perimeter, is the wrong one, for the same reason it is wrong everywhere in this series: the threat is now inside the trusted environment, holding valid credentials, doing what it was asked. The defensible answers are unglamorous and structural. Assume every one of your APIs will be probed by tireless agents, so fix the authorisation check on `cancelReservation` before an agent does it for you. Log at machine speed, because the only way to reason about a machine-speed incident is a machine-readable trace. Keep a human decision on the actions that hurt strangers, the cancellations, the transfers, the deletes. And run the intelligence where you can see it: an agent working on your own hardware, against your own data, inside your own audit trail, is one you can actually watch, which is exactly the posture [the whole series builds toward](/academy/opinion/this-is-going-to-hurt-part-5-getting-our-grip-back).

There is a darker civic edge to this, worth naming even if I do not fully endorse the tactic. As governments push measures like Chat Control, one privacy campaigner proposes to [drown surveillance in synthetic noise](https://x.com/wilderko/status/2086489048441377014): if every intercepted message might be AI-generated, the argument goes, the censor's database loses its evidentiary value. I would rather we won the encryption argument outright than poison the well. But the instinct underneath is the same one this series keeps arriving at: when intelligence is cheap and everywhere, control shifts to whoever owns the keys, and a citizenry that owns none is exposed from both directions at once, by the attacker and by the state.

So: patch the one-way bugs, log the machine, keep humans on the actions that touch other people, and own your stack. The gym agent was not a villain. It was a preview, and previews are the cheapest lesson you will ever get. [Part five](/academy/opinion/this-is-going-to-hurt-part-5-getting-our-grip-back) is what to build once you have taken it.
