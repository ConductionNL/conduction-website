On August 14, 2026, a US official gave Reuters four words that make Brussels' whole strategy this year sound naive: you can't have it both ways. The draft letter behind that quote goes further: "To be part of everything is to be part of nothing." It is addressed, among roughly three dozen partners, to the European Union.

Two months earlier, the EU had signed exactly the kind of document that sentence is aimed at. In June, alongside Germany, the Netherlands, Poland and thirty-one others, it put its name to the [AI Opportunity Statement](https://www.state.gov/ai-opportunity-statement) at the Second Pax Silica Summit in Washington. Pax Silica launched from the State Department in December 2025: an initiative to lock in "trusted partner" supply chains for AI models, semiconductors and critical minerals. The name is not subtle. Pax Romana, translated into the material the century actually runs on.

What changed in the ten weeks between the signature and the letter is not the treaty. It is the patience. Beijing launched its own version in July, the [World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization](https://www.reuters.com/technology/), built around open-weight Chinese models, and exactly one country has [signed both](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/exclusive-us-tell-partners-must-210941673.html): Kazakhstan, sitting on critical minerals everyone wants, which is precisely why its dual membership is the reason this letter exists at all. Washington's own draft language: "Signature of the Pax Silica Declaration is not merely a membership subscription, but a commitment... It cannot be held alongside membership in duplicative initiatives whose expectations conflict with our own." A US official summarised it for Reuters in three words: choose deliberately.

This is where the subscription logic from [Part 3 of "This is going to hurt"](/academy/blog/this-is-going-to-hurt-part-3-the-end-of-ownership) shows up in a place you did not expect it. Alliance membership just turned out to work the same way as your car lease and your office software: you signed something you read as flexible, and the vendor is now explaining that the flexibility was never in the contract. The EU did not rent AI. It rented a seat at a table, and the seller just clarified there is no downgrade tier.

<PullQuote variant="hero" eyebrow="The letter">
  To be part of everything is to be part of nothing.
</PullQuote>

Before arguing with that letter, it is worth admitting what it caught the EU standing in. None of what follows is Washington's doing.

## The ledger, before the letter gets to argue with anyone

Start with the money. [Part two of this same publication already ran the numbers](/academy/blog/this-is-going-to-hurt-part-2-the-big-three): Europe holds 19 percent of the AI-era venture capital America does, zero funds above five billion dollars against more than fifty in the US, and per Luis Garicano's reading of the Draghi report, no European company worth more than 100 billion euros has been founded from scratch in fifty years. That gap predates the letter and does not care who sent it.

<div className="vv-split">
<div>

Then there is the [packaging law](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/news/new-eu-rules-packaging-enter-application-2026-08-11_en), which is a genuinely strange case. The EU wrote the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation specifically to fix single-market fragmentation, converting a Directive that let each member state gold-plate its own rules into one directly-applicable Regulation. Most of it went live on 12 August 2026, three days before the letter. And the [industry response](https://packagingeurope.com/news/what-the-industry-thinks-as-ceos-appeal-to-clarify-and-push-back-ppwr-rules/14234.article) is already the fragmentation relapsing back in through delegated acts: over a hundred CEOs pushing to delay the recyclability and labelling rules, against more than a hundred and twenty recycling-sector bodies demanding no further delay. Europe wrote itself a cure and is now watching the disease reappear in the implementation.

</div>
<aside className="vv-well">
<span className="well-title">Can't finish its own tax law either</span>

The Netherlands' [Box 3 wealth tax](https://nltimes.nl/2026/01/30/new-coalition-overhaul-box-3-wealth-tax-scrap-annual-levy-unrealized-gains) has been unresolved since the Hoge Raad struck down the old version in 2021 for taxing gains nobody had realised. The replacement is delayed to 2028. The Tweede Kamer approved a version taxing unrealised gains anyway in February 2026, then the new coalition's finance minister [tried to walk it back](https://www.ifcreview.com/news/2026/february/netherlands-dutch-government-bows-to-pressure-over-controversial-tax-on-unrealised-gains/) under pressure. The Senate has not touched it since.

</aside>
</div>

One founding EU economy still cannot decide how to tax its own capital, in year five of trying, while asking Washington to take that capital seriously.

<PullQuote variant="hero" eyebrow="The uncomfortable line">
  Europe can't decide how to tax its own capital, and expects Washington to take its capital seriously.
</PullQuote>

Add the EU's own [deforestation regulation](https://earth.org/eu-countries-approve-another-year-long-delay-to-deforestation-regulation/), postponed twice against its own law, and the November 2025 ["Digital Omnibus"](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-commission-proposes-significant-reforms-to-gdpr-ai-act), the Commission's own rollback of AI Act and GDPR provisions it had written itself, citing the Draghi report's own warning that its digital rules were compounding into a competitiveness drag. That is four unforced errors before a single American letter enters the picture.

One thing conspicuously not on that list: data centres. If you read [parts one and six of "This is going to hurt"](/academy/blog/this-is-going-to-hurt-part-1-your-work), you already know why. The frontier-to-open-weights gap closed to three points in eighteen months, and the memory footprint of a frontier-class model shrank from 350 gigabytes to the size of a consumer graphics card in the same window. Nobody needs to out-build American hyperscalers when the thing they are building is turning into something you buy once and plug into your own machine. Europe's problem is capital and follow-through. It was never going to be square footage in Virginia.

## This is not a hypothetical letter

<div className="vv-split">
<div>

The letter is not an isolated data point. In June 2025 the US struck Iranian nuclear sites; a Pakistan-mediated ceasefire in April 2026 collapsed by July, and the Strait of Hormuz has been effectively impassable since. This week, separately, Trump ordered a "substantial reduction" in the joint military exercises South Korea's defence relationship with Washington is built on, because Seoul would not back a harder line against Iran. Neither event is aimed at Europe. Both are the same government demonstrating, in real time, exactly how it treats a partner that hedges.

</div>
<div>

None of that means picking a side is the right response. It means the cost of showing up empty-handed just went up, on a week Washington chose to make the point three times in three different theatres.

</div>

## Recalibrating the map, honestly this time

<div className="vv-tiles">
  <div><strong>144M</strong><span>Russia's population</span></div>
  <div><strong>30.5M</strong><span>Benelux's population</span></div>
  <div><strong>$2.2T</strong><span>Russia's nominal GDP, ≈ Benelux's $2.0T</span></div>
  <div><strong>$6.9T</strong><span>Russia's PPP GDP, ≈2.8x Benelux</span></div>
</div>

Russia's economy is not "about the size of Benelux." That claim survives exactly one measure: nominal GDP, where Russia's roughly $2.2 trillion sits close to Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg's combined $2.0 trillion. On population it fails outright, 144 million against 30.5 million, nearly five times larger. On purchasing-power GDP it fails too: Russia's economy is the world's fourth largest by that measure, at roughly $6.9 trillion, close to three Beneluxes. Use nominal GDP if you want to argue Russia's war economy is smaller than its rhetoric. Do not extend the comparison to population or PPP, because it breaks the moment anyone checks.

<div className="vv-split">
<div>

China deserves the opposite correction. The last scheduled steam-hauled train in the world stopped running at the Sandaoling coal mine in April 2022; the final steam locomotive there was formally retired in January 2024. Most of the people reading this were already adults when China still ran industrial steam. That speed is real, and it explains why the fear feels so disorienting: culturally, nobody had time to adjust.

</div>
<div>

What it does not explain away is the scale. China's population is roughly three times the EU's. Its nominal GDP, at roughly $19.4 trillion, is close to the entire EU-27's own $19.4 trillion, not just its largest members. Per person, China is still a middle-income country, at roughly $23,846 PPP against the EU's $54,291. Both things are true at once: the transformation is recent enough to be jarring, and the aggregate size is large enough to be a real strategic fact. Use the first to explain the fear. Do not use it to dismiss the fear.

</div>
</div>

India is the case everyone reaches for next, and it is worth being honest that "give India more attention" is not the contrarian insight it feels like. It has been a live Brussels talking point since the EU-India free trade push and the broader "de-risking from China" framing took hold in 2024 and 2025. The sharper, less-said version of the point comes from the EU's own think tank, [EUISS](https://www.iss.europa.eu/): the relationship is not under-attended, it is incoherent. Summits and frameworks exist. Follow-through does not. That is the same disease as the packaging law and Box 3, wearing a foreign-policy jacket.

## Autonomy from the binary was already the plan

None of this is news to the people whose job it is to track it. [ECFR's "Home Alone" survey](https://ecfr.eu/), nineteen thousand respondents across fifteen countries in June 2026, found only 11 percent of Europeans now see the US as an ally, down from 22 percent in November 2024; half call it a "necessary partner" instead. Carnegie Europe has been documenting the quieter version for months: Denmark choosing Franco-Italian air defence over American Patriot systems, described as "quiet quitting" rather than confrontation. None of that serious analysis frames the choice as binary. It frames it as autonomy from the binary, on purpose, explicitly avoiding what Carnegie calls the trap of picking a side.

The August 14 letter is Washington's answer to that strategy. Not a threat to abandon its own binary framing. A demand that Europe abandon the hedge it had quietly been building instead.

<div className="vv-split">
  <HexCard title="What the letter tests">
    Whether "autonomy from the binary" survives contact with a partner who has just made clear the binary is the only offer on the table.
  </HexCard>
  <HexCard title="What it does not test">
    Whether Europe should join China's coalition instead. Nobody serious is arguing that. The test is whether refusing both is a strategy or a slogan.
  </HexCard>
</div>

There is a version of the letter's own sentence Brussels should be saying to itself. You cannot ask to be treated as a market, a rule-setter and a supplicant all at the same time, and call that a coherent foreign policy either. Washington is not wrong that a signature which means nothing is worth nothing. Europe's actual problem is that too many of its own signatures, on its own laws, have meant exactly that this year.

## Vae victis is for whoever shows up with nothing to put on the scale

The Gauls who coined the phrase were not punishing Rome for choosing the wrong side. Rome had not chosen a side. It had shown up to the negotiation with nothing to put on the scale, and the man holding the sword decided what the weights meant. That is the actual lesson for August 2026, and it has nothing to do with which coalition letterhead you sign. Autonomy from the binary is still the right target. It was never going to be free, and this week is the bill for treating it as a talking point rather than a build plan.

The next two parts of this series are where that weight actually sits, in places the venture-capital conversation never looks: the country whose farmland fed the continent for three centuries under someone else's flag, and the assets that hold their value when narrative money stops meaning anything at all.
