Open source projects do not die from a lack of code. They die from governance. More precisely: from the mismatch between the culture that produces working software and the culture that produces committees.

{/* truncate */}

I have spent twenty years in and around Dutch government IT, and I have sat across from more steering groups than I can count. Never in them. Across from them, fighting them, usually as the one who just wanted to ship. In all those years I never saw a steering group make a project faster. Slower, yes. That is what a steering group does. It absorbs risk by adding process, and sometimes that is what you need. In a technical community that runs on volunteer energy, it is usually how the dying starts.

You know the two-wolves meme. Inside every governmental open source project there are also two wolves. One writes code. The other writes policy. The code wolf wants to ship this week. The policy wolf wants to be able to explain, eighteen months from now, why nothing that went wrong was its fault. The entire art of open source governance is stopping those two from eating each other alive. Hardly anyone manages it, and this weekend the Nix community delivered the textbook demonstration of what it looks like when the policy wolf wins.

## Ten months, two signatures, one applicant

On 8 August the [Nixpkgs Core Team announced it was disbanding](https://discourse.nixos.org/t/the-nixpkgs-core-team-has-disbanded/79413), roughly ten months after it was formed. The announcement is short and worth reading in full. The role, the two remaining members wrote, had "not turned out to be the lightweight role compatible with active technical contribution that we had originally hoped". Stepping down was "necessary for our health". Two volunteers walked off the field because staying was hurting them, while they cared about the game as much as ever. In a movement that runs entirely on people choosing to show up, there is no worse outcome than that. A public call for new members produced exactly one applicant.

The sharper part is structural. The team wrote that the NixOS Steering Committee "lacks a native instinct for the delegation envisioned by the constitution". In practice: unnecessary micromanagement of teams, chronically poor communication, no way to tell whether a committee member was speaking personally or officially, and, in their words, "general uncertainty about whether we are trusted to autonomously make decisions". [Steering Committee member John Ericson pushed back](https://discourse.nixos.org/t/the-scs-role-in-the-disbandment-of-the-nixpkgs-core-team/79433), calling the micromanagement claim "objectively false" and pointing out that the Core Team had barely appeared on the committee's agenda.

A project with a written constitution, elected bodies and formally delegated mandates produced two groups of capable people who cannot even agree on whether interference took place. When governance works, that debate does not exist. The disagreement is the diagnosis.

The people inside the project are saying the quiet part out loud now. Former NixOS release manager Jon Ringer: ["FOSS nerds don't make good politicians… Nix governance was set up to fail."](https://x.com/jonringer117/status/2086116977974952114) A sitting Steering Committee member, [@cafkafk](https://x.com/cafkafk/status/2086532204276924802): "NixOS is such a correct and strong idea that it will survive our absolutely dysfunctional governance. The institution is fine. The people just fail it." Even the defence concedes the dysfunction. The only dispute left is whether to blame the org chart or the humans inside it, which is the same dispute the Core Team and Ericson are having, and it does not matter who wins it. A governance layer that requires better humans than the ones it actually has is a failed design.

And this was not a first. Anyone who followed Nix through [2024](https://lwn.net/Articles/971531/) knows the pattern: the Anduril sponsorship rows, the ["Save Nix Together" open letter](https://github.com/save-nix-together/open-letter/blob/main/content/posts/letter.md), [Eelco Dolstra stepping away from the Foundation board](https://discourse.nixos.org/t/leaving-the-foundation-going-back-to-the-roots/46644), [moderation teams resigning](https://itsfoss.gitlab.io/post/nixos-moderation-team-resigns-in-protest-of-sc-interference/) in waves. Every crisis produced more structure. Structure does not solve problems; people do. Here the structure itself was the problem, and every round of repairs made it bigger. Four days before the disbandment, long-time contributor Domen Kožar published a piece arguing that [Nixpkgs has a due process problem](https://domenkozar.com/2026/08/04/nixpkgs-has-a-due-process-problem/), with a line every governance designer should frame: "The test of governance is not whether it gives me what I want. It is whether I would accept the same rules if the people and positions were reversed."

Nixpkgs itself is fine. The packages still get reviewed, the build farm still runs, and thousands of contributors shipped right through the drama. What collapsed was a governance layer, and the work continued without it. Read that again.

## Europe has run this experiment before

Battlestar Galactica had a liturgy for it: all of this has happened before, and all of this will happen again. If the Nix case feels like an internal community affair, look at what happens when governments run the same play at continental scale. Europe keeps launching sovereign infrastructure vehicles, and as [one observer on X noted while listing them](https://x.com/_billiambennett/status/2074156226502435237), from Theseus, the German web-search moonshot of the 2000s, through Bull and the European Processor Initiative to Gaia-X, there is "surprisingly little public conversation on the past failure modes".

**Gaia-X** is the monument. Announced in 2019 and unveiled by the French and German economy ministers as the project that would restore Europe's digital sovereignty, it grew past 320 members and admitted the very players it was meant to counter as full members: Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Huawei, Alibaba, and, yes, really, Palantir. By October 2021, sixteen months after the unveiling, [Politico had more than a dozen insiders on record describing infighting, a bloated structure and postponed deadlines; one industry official simply called it "a mess"](https://www.politico.eu/article/chaos-and-infighting-are-killing-europes-grand-cloud-project/). Frank Karlitschek of Nextcloud, then a member: "There's too many cooks in the kitchen." Yann Lechelle, at the time CEO of Scaleway, watched the hyperscalers work the structure: "First, they knock on the door, then they take a step in the door ... Very quickly it became clear that these guys were dominant in the technical groups." Later he was blunter still: ["The committees were drowning ... Americans have full-time lobbyists and massive budgets. Their job is basically to derail any initiative they don't like."](https://www.politico.eu/article/anatomy-franco-german-tech-misfire-american-dependence/) While Gaia-X deliberated, those same players ran 69 percent of the European cloud market. Europe's largest provider, Deutsche Telekom, held two.

Seven years in, [Gaia-X has built no data centre and sells no cloud service](https://en.ilsole24ore.com/art/europes-big-bet-on-the-cloud-remains-without-a-data-centre-AInU9xxD); what it produces is labels, rules, compliance frameworks and ontologies. Even its flagship success story, the farming-data hub Agdatahub, went bankrupt. Its own former CEO Francesco Bonfiglio summed up his tenure as being ["the CEO of a consensus-based organization where consensus couldn't be achieved most of the time"](https://www.politico.eu/article/anatomy-franco-german-tech-misfire-american-dependence/). And: "I joined Gaia-X because I believed in the original mission. I left Gaia-X because I didn't believe it was going in the original direction." Bert Hubert's technical verdict is the one that sticks: Gaia-X ended up producing ["standards for talking about standards", two steps removed from being useful](https://berthub.eu/articles/posts/gaia-x-is-an-expensive-distraction/). Lechelle, who quit in 2021 and calls it the best decision he ever made, concluded: "those who wanted to maintain the status quo have won."

<PullQuote attribution="Yann Lechelle" role="Gaia-X founding member, former CEO Scaleway">
  A crushing failure, a colossal waste of time, and just as many years gained for the hyperscalers. In other words: an industrial disaster.
</PullQuote>

On X the epitaphs are shorter. ["If you want to laugh go read up on Gaia-X.. the EU's attempt at its own AWS, total shit show and disaster as you can imagine."](https://x.com/PositivFuturist/status/2004475160808898743) ["Gaia-X failed mainly because it focused on creating complexity instead of delivering value."](https://x.com/kuko_b/status/1992620469800046690) And the driest one: ["Talk is cheap. Infrastructure is not."](https://x.com/Mr_Hydrate/status/2015973574822023586) Seven years of summits, and the sharpest analyses of the project fit in a tweet.

Europe is about to spend billions on the next round, so the lesson needs to be precise. Standards bodies and governance structures do not win battles against the hyperscalers. They hand them ammunition: every committee seat, every consensus requirement, every postponed policy paper was a lever the incumbents pulled to slow everything down, from the inside, at Europe's expense. People win these battles. Governance loses them.

**La Suite Numérique** shows the other failure mode: the state as builder. France's DINUM builds its own messaging, video and collaboration tools for civil servants, fully publicly funded. The Cour des Comptes went through the previous generation of those tools in 2024 and the numbers are brutal: [9.3 million euros spent on Tchap, Resana and Osmose by the end of 2023, five million a year in maintenance, for tools that remained little used and unknown to most civil servants](https://www.zdnet.fr/blogs/l-esprit-libre/concurrence-irresponsable-le-cnll-pilonne-la-dinum-396752.htm). Tchap, the state-built messenger, absorbed most of that budget, and the story ended with France's own Prime Minister recommending civil servants use Olvid, a proprietary alternative. The CNLL, the French open source industry association, called the whole posture "concurrence irresponsable", irresponsible competition, and put it sharply: DINUM plays against its own side. A dozen French vendors already ship collaborative tooling (Murena, XWiki, BlueMind, Jamespot and others, some of them state-funded), while the state rebuilds their products with 100 percent public financing, the state's marketing power, and none of the SecNumCloud obligations its competitors carry. And the [auditors' proposed remedy? Give DINUM more authority](https://www.republik-it.fr/decideurs-it/gouvernance/chronique-quand-la-cour-des-comptes-reclame-une-dinum-plus-autoritaire.html). When the only tool you trust is control, every failure looks like a shortage of it.

The sequel is running right now. In January the responsible minister announced that Visio, DINUM's in-house videoconferencing tool, [will be imposed on every state service by 2027](https://www.lemagit.fr/actualites/366638848/Polemique-autour-de-LaSuite-la-DINUM-se-defend-et-ne-ferme-pas-la-porte-au-prive), and the French vendors went to war. Renaud Ghia of Tixeo: the state pays twice, first subsidising private R&D through France 2030 and the research tax credit, then funding an internal team to build the competing product. Alain Garnier of Jamespot: by trying to produce everything itself, the state weakens the very ecosystem it claims to support. DINUM's defence, that Visio and LaSuite are "digital commons" rather than commercial offers, would carry more weight if the dozen French vendors in that exact market had been asked first. Garnier's summary is the correct division of labour in one sentence: a state that architects the foundation, not a state that becomes a general-purpose software vendor.

**18F** proves that even success does not protect you. The United States GSA unit practised open-by-default development for eleven years and shipped things that worked: [IRS Direct File, Login.gov, a design system used by nearly two hundred federal sites](https://theconversation.com/how-18f-transformed-government-technology-and-why-its-elimination-matters-251333). On 1 March 2025 it was eliminated as "non-critical", its staff laid off, no succession plan. The irony arrived by statute. Congress had just passed the SHARE IT Act, requiring every agency to share its custom-developed code, when the administration erased the one team that had shown agencies how. ["There's no resource, with 18F gone and USDS functionally gone, for agencies to draw on to help comply with this law,"](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/it-modernization/2025/03/gov-tech-layoffs-could-hamper-share-it-act-software-mandates/) former 18F technologist Waldo Jaquith told Federal News Network. A demonstrated, working capability for government open source erased in one political risk calculation, four months after a law started depending on it.

**LibreOffice** adds the newest exhibit. In June 2025 [The Document Foundation's board terminated the memberships of more than thirty developers](https://lwn.net/Articles/1066418/) employed by Collabora and allotropia, the companies behind roughly eighty percent of commits to the codebase, over alleged conflicts of interest. Seven of the top ten committers of all time, longtime architect Michael Meeks among them, [ejected from the foundation built around their own work](https://www.collaboraonline.com/blog/tdf-ejects-its-core-developers/). Collabora called it ["a brazen move to consolidate power": "The Board has chosen to eject the people who actually build LibreOffice."](https://www.webpronews.com/the-document-foundation-just-expelled-the-engineers-who-built-libreoffice-and-the-fallout-could-reshape-open-source-governance/) The money makes it uglier. The foundation collects an estimated five million euros or more a year in donations and growing reserves, given by users who believe they are funding LibreOffice development, while the engineering is paid for by the two companies the board just expelled. Collabora is now reducing its involvement and building a cut-down suite of its own. And the industry has stopped waiting: in March, [Nextcloud, IONOS, Proton and others launched Euro-Office](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4152487/euro-office-billed-as-europes-sovereign-alternative-to-microsoft-office.html), a European fork of OnlyOffice, with Collabora code flowing in. The Document Foundation's response was to brand Euro-Office a ["de facto ally" of Microsoft](https://www.omgubuntu.co.uk/2026/06/euro-office-ooxml-libreoffice): a governance layer now at war with its own engineers and with the market's answer to the mess it made. Remember: LibreOffice exists because the OpenOffice.org community fled Oracle's stewardship, and the fork conditions are assembling again. The board that remains holds the brand, the bank account and the trademark. Trademarks do not compile code.

The small cases rhyme with the big ones. LWN's write-up of [two decades of Mexican government open source](https://lwn.net/Articles/1013776) contains the most honest sentence in this whole genre, from practitioner González Waite: "liability could get you into jail, so don't change the technology." And this spring the UK's NHS, in an internal guidance note [surfaced by Terence Eden](https://www.neowin.net/news/is-this-the-end-nhs-is-apparently-shutting-down-most-of-its-open-source-repos-heres-why), ordered all source code repositories private by default: "Public repositories materially increase the risk of unintended disclosure ... particularly given rapid advancements in AI models capable of large-scale code ingestion, inference, and reasoning." A senior insider summarised it as "changing our tack on coding in the open". Different countries, different decades, same reflex: when in doubt, add control, reduce exposure, slow down.

## Why this lands on your desk

Back to Nix, because it stopped being a hobbyist curiosity a while ago. France's DINUM is [building its exit from Windows on NixOS](https://www.thestack.technology/frances-shift-from-us-technology-starts-with-250-dinum-machines-and-nixos/): [Sécurix](https://github.com/cloud-gouv/securix/blob/main/README.md), a hardened configuration for system administrators built to ANSSI requirements with hardware-key logins, and Bureautix for regular office work. Two hundred and fifty machines by the end of 2026, ministry-by-ministry roadmaps after that. Reproducible builds, declarative configuration and full operator control are the properties everyone in the Eurostack conversations says they want when the alternative is an opaque cloud subject to foreign law.

DINUM plays both roles in this story. With La Suite it builds its own team and crowds out a market. With NixOS it consumes and adapts a living open project. Watch which of the two produces adoption.

So European public money is about to stand on a project whose governance just ate its own middle layer. I know what happens when that sentence gets read in a ministry, because I have watched the reflex for twenty years: this project clearly needs proper governance. A steering group, a decision process, a risk register, a stakeholder matrix. The mechanisms a government trusts, because they are the mechanisms a government is made of. Gaia-X had all of them. That is the reflex to resist.

## The referee on an empty field

Let's be honest about the underlying mismatch. A government optimises for not being blamed. Every decision documented, every risk mitigated, every stakeholder consulted, every outcome predictable. Inside a ministry that is rational; it is what democratic accountability costs. An open source community at its best optimises for shipping working systems. It tolerates ambiguity, rewards fast iteration, accepts that some calls will be wrong, and moves on. A steering group that must stay defensible to auditors will choose the safe, slow option every single time. In a package repository that has to absorb thousands of contributions and settle disputes in days, safe-and-slow bleeds people.

I made this argument earlier this year in an internal advisory for a Dutch public-sector collaboration wrestling with exactly this question. Both cultures are valid for the organisation that carries them. An open community runs bottom-up: the people doing the work get the trust and the room to shape it, and that trust is precisely the source of the quality everyone praises. A government runs on risk control, and that is just as functional: its actions face public and political scrutiny, and its value lies in predictability and care. Room to move in a ministry does not come from freedom for professionals; it comes from accountability processes and steering groups. Put sharply: a government project is allowed to fail, as long as everyone reported neatly and the right bodies took the decisions. That is how a government delivers the predictability we ask of it.

What the two cultures cannot do is share one programme. It is in neither side's interest to give up its own culture, or to ask the other to bend. Try it anyway and you get the worst of both worlds: experts who report instead of build, and administrators accountable for a process they cannot actually steer. One interviewee in that advisory put it in a single line: if you don't have the culture, you can't join. A community like Nixpkgs runs on informal, craft-based norms: you know each other, you ship code, you trust each other's judgment, and disputes end in a merged patch. Someone arriving from an accountability culture has the will and the knowledge, and still misses the assumptions underneath. They go looking for a steering group where a pull request is the answer. Money does not close that gap either; I have watched a government rebuild with three times the community's budget sink anyway, because it was welded to the wrong context.

<PullQuote variant="hero" eyebrow="The uncomfortable observation">
  No project has ever gone faster because of a steering group. Slowing down is what steering groups are for.
</PullQuote>

Governance without players is a referee on an empty field. That is the picture every story above ends on. A match needs players before it needs anything else. A good referee makes the game better, somebody has to call the fouls. But nobody in the stadium came for the referee. The failure mode of open source governance looks like a football club where the officials are in a back room refining the VAR protocol, the referee is standing in position on the pitch, the audience is still cheering out of habit, and the players have quietly left, because the fun went out of the game somewhere between the third committee and the fourth postponed deadline. Gaia-X is that stadium at continental scale. Nixpkgs just showed us the miniature: two players walked off on 8 August and told us exactly why on their way out.

And if you work for a public administration that is betting on open source infrastructure, this is about you. The moment something becomes strategically important, your organisation will want to own it, and owning it will mean installing the mechanisms it understands. Install those on a technical community and you hang your process weight on the handful of people who do the actual work. They burn out or leave, the way the Nixpkgs Core Team just did and the way Collabora's engineers are doing now. New contributors hesitate. Decision latency climbs. In the worst case the governance itself becomes the next crisis, which then demands more governance.

## What to do instead

Good open source governance asks one question before any other: what keeps the players on the field? Everything else is secondary. Some things genuinely need clear ownership, security response, trademarks, money, and fine, give those a formal home. Keep that list short and keep it out of the engineering flow.

Money is a large part of the answer, and here governments currently make things worse twice over. They finance their own teams: La Suite gets full public funding while the companies of the CNLL compete against their own state. And for the amateur club, the community project that actually has players on the pitch, entering the competition is nearly impossible: procurement is written for clubs with a licensed stadium, an insurance file and a legal department. The maintainers who keep Nixpkgs running, the engineers behind LibreOffice, the small vendors behind half the Eurostack: they are the ones public money should reach, and they are the ones it structurally misses.

So invert it. Fund the people who already do the work, without attaching control rights, because a funding line with a board seat creates the same dual power structure Nix just showed us failing. Write procurement that rewards upstream health instead of local forks or a government-stamped "version". Accept that rough consensus among active contributors is a legitimate way to decide things, even though no auditor will ever love it. And build your own capacity to consume, package and adapt the software, because that is the part you can control. Sécurix is the proof that this posture works: France did not demand a seat on any Nix board, it took the project as it is and built its hardening on top, under its own culture, with its own accountability processes. The community does not have to start reporting. The government does not have to start improvising.

Someone on X [compressed the whole sovereign-infrastructure lesson into four words](https://x.com/meghanatweets/status/2066593382164468020): "Don't crown. Layer." The same post lined up Project Independence, Gaia-X and Aleph Alpha and closed the case: "Three countries, three decades, three failure modes. All started with the press release." So stop anointing a single chosen vehicle with a board and a launch event, and start layering: adopt what runs, contribute where it hurts, harden what you depend on, and let the market and the community carry what you started. Every failure on the list above began with a coronation. Every quiet success, from Sécurix to [SURF's 100,000-user Nextcloud rollout](/academy/opinion/government-open-source-needs-an-engine), began with a layer.

  <Outcome>Fund maintainers directly, with no board seat or veto attached to the money</Outcome>
  <Outcome>Open your procurement to the amateur clubs: make it possible for community projects and small vendors to enter and stay in the competition</Outcome>
  <Outcome>Put upstream health into your procurement criteria and reward contribution over forking</Outcome>
  <Outcome>Build internal capacity to consume and adapt the software; leave day-to-day direction to the people who ship it</Outcome>
  <Outcome>When a crisis really does need structure, give that structure an end date</Outcome>
</Outcomes>

Nixpkgs built formal governance in response to real crises, and then watched the layer in the middle burn out under the friction that structure generated. The packages survived because enough people still care about the technical work. That is the part worth protecting, and it is the one part a steering group cannot produce.

Most policy ends its life in a drawer. That is fine; drawers are where policy retires. The sovereignty strategies, the cloud frameworks, the coalition paragraphs on open source, most of them will join the Gaia-X position papers in the archive, and Europe will survive that. What Europe will not survive is letting them take our open source future into the drawer with them: the maintainers who burned out waiting for a mandate, the vendors that gave up competing against their own state, the projects that spent their best years in committee.

Fund the work. Trust the people doing it. Keep the players on the field.
