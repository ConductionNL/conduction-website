The honest answer starts with three history lessons, because history is the only thing that has ever calibrated a shift this fast. Start with the one that stings the most.

## You are the horse, and the horse peaked in 1915

<div className="hfaw-split">
<div>

American horses and mules peaked at roughly 26.5 million around 1915, fifteen years after the automobile arrived, and then fell to 3 million by 1960. Cars went from nothing to 8 million by 1920 and 61.7 million by 1960.

Read the marker position again: the incumbent was at its strongest the moment it was already finished. Nobody at the 1915 horse fair experienced decline; demand had never been higher.

  The horse population peaked fifteen years after the car arrived. Substitution never looks like substitution from the saddle.

That is what the middle of a substitution feels like from the inside: busier than ever. Your inbox is fuller, your backlog longer, the demand for your reports, designs, translations and code reviews at an all-time high. None of that is evidence the transition missed you. The horse had record employment in 1915.

## Eras overlap more than the history books admit

<div className="hfaw-split">
<div>

New eras do not wait for old ones to finish. Morse sent the first telegram in 1844. By 1861 Lincoln was running a war by wire from the War Department's telegraph office. And in 1877, while the telegraph was already wiring the planet together, Saigō Takamori died in the Satsuma Rebellion as the last samurai.

Your organisation is that timeline in miniature. Somewhere in your building an intern is shipping working software by directing three AI agents, while two floors up a committee is scheduling the Q3 evaluation of whether to pilot a chatbot. Both are real, both are now, and the distance between them is not time. It is adoption.

## Ten times faster than your gut

<div className="hfaw-split">
<div>

Your intuition about how fast technology arrives was trained on the wrong century. The telephone took seventy years to reach half of American homes. Electricity took forty-three. The web did it in ten, the smartphone in six. ChatGPT reached 800 million weekly users in four.

Sixty-six years separated Kitty Hawk from the Moon: one human lifetime spans the first powered flight and the first footprint up there. That was the fast century. The current one compressed a comparable leap into about a year, the time between two Nextcloud LTS releases. When someone in a meeting says "this will take a generation", they are quoting the telephone's schedule to a technology that runs on the web's.

## The robots already clocked in. The question is when you clock out

The abstract version of this is easy to wave away. The concrete version is filming itself. Walk into an Albert Heijn after closing and a [Pudu cleaning robot is scrubbing the floor](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/pudurobotics_retail-puducc1-pudurobotics-activity-7322560227034177537-rkXR) that a person mopped last year. Amazon's warehouses now run [more than a million robots](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8-ESlUGAwGU); its Sparrow arm already handles about 65 percent of the distinct items it sells, and its newest machine, Blue Jay, does picking, stowing and consolidating in one unit. Waymo runs paid driverless taxis across several US cities. Your neighbour's lawn is cut by a robot that never asks for a Saturday. None of these is a demo reel from a lab; they are line items in operating budgets, filmed by amused shoppers and posted to TikTok.

The same pattern the horse showed repeats one layer down, in the physical world right now: the routine and predictable goes first, whether it is a mop, a picking arm or a steering wheel. And the exposure is not evenly spread. The studies that try to measure it keep landing on the same shape.

<div className="hfaw-split">
<div>

Task-exposure estimates cluster around the desk jobs first, not the trades: administrative support near [46 percent](https://www.demandsage.com/ai-job-replacement-stats/), legal and financial operations not far behind, and up to 65 percent of routine retail tasks in reach. The OECD puts 27 percent of jobs at high automation risk across its member countries. And the load is uneven in a way that should bother us: in high-income countries the most-exposed roles make up nearly three times the share of women's jobs as men's. This is not a rounding error landing on everyone equally. It lands hardest on the routine desk work that a generation was told was the safe choice.

The through-line to the desk worker reading this: "physical and routine" was never a synonym for "someone else". The mop went first because it was routine, not because it was physical. The routine parts of your day are next in line for the same reason.

## The market already repriced your toolbox

<div className="hfaw-split">
<div>

If you want to know what the money thinks AI does to knowledge work, look at what happened to the software you use every day. In the spring of 2026 the market staged what analysts now call the [SaaS-pocalypse](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/04/09/the-saas-pocalypses-biggest-losers-figma-duolingo-and-monday-com-suffer-brutal-meltdowns/): Figma down 86.5 percent from its high, Duolingo down 83.3, Monday.com down 80.2, against 35 percent for the broad software index.

Here is the part that matters for your work: the businesses were fine. Figma grew revenue 48 percent year over year and beat every estimate; Duolingo posted 52.7 million daily users and real profit. What collapsed was the belief that a tool which charges per human seat survives a world where the work inside the seat gets automated. The market is not pricing what these companies earn. It is pricing what happens to the tasks, and [some analysts already call the sell-off overdone](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/05/03/goldman-sachs-says-the-artificial-intelligence-ai/), which tells you how unresolved the question still is. Either way, the repricing of your toolbox has already happened. The repricing of your task list is next.

## What adapted work already looks like

You do not have to imagine the post-transition workplace; pockets of it already run in production. Google studied its own code review process across [nine million reviewed changes](https://x.com/milan_milanovic/status/2086702090722877833), and the numbers describe a different physics of work. The median change is 24 lines, and more than ten percent of changes touch a single line. Seventy percent is committed within 24 hours of going out for review, and the median review takes under four hours from start to end, against 15 to 24 hours at comparable companies. A developer spends 3.2 hours a week reviewing. The tooling absorbs the boring part: static analysis runs before a human ever looks at the change, an attention set shows whose turn it is, and when a reviewer suggests a fix the author applies it with one click, increasingly with the machine drafting the edit itself. One cultural rule does more than any feature: a reviewer cannot reject work and walk away, because every piece of negative feedback has to point at something specific to fix. The result is a number most teams will not recognise: 97 percent of Google's engineers are satisfied with their review process.

The lesson travels far beyond code review. The unit of work shrank until one human could hold it in their head, the machine took over the mechanical checking, and human judgement moved to the centre of a fast loop instead of the end of a slow one. A 24-line change reviews in an hour; a 900-line change waits for days, and the difference is not the process but the size. Every knowledge-work profession has its version of the 900-line change, and the teams that thrive next to AI are the ones that learn to work in 24-line units, with the model doing the linting and a person doing the judging.

Even the crankiest workshop on the internet shows the same shape. Linus Torvalds calls the upcoming [Linux kernel 7.2 release "huge"](https://x.com/Itsfoss/status/2086770941670109187), not for ambitious features but because AI tools are reviewing kernel code at a pace human contributors cannot match: "the new normal with a lot of fixes, many of them due to review by various AI tools." The same Torvalds spent the start of this year complaining that AI-generated bug reports had made the kernel's security list "almost entirely unmanageable", and he still finds AI patches poorly documented and hard to review. Both statements are true at once, and together they are the honest state of AI at work: a flood of cheap output wherever nobody filters, real acceleration wherever judgement stays in the loop. His own summary: "Linux is not one of those anti-AI projects." If the most famously unimpressed reviewer alive is making his peace with the new normal, the committee on your second floor can too.

And the org chart is starting to follow the workflow. Satya Nadella described at Davos [what LinkedIn just did to four of its job titles](https://x.com/karlmehta/status/2086075045462540293): product manager, designer, front-end engineer and back-end engineer, merged into one role with a bigger scope, the full-stack builder, working a new loop that starts with evals instead of ending with a check. LinkedIn's famous Associate Product Manager programme is finished; its replacement track teaches code, design and product management at the same time. Whatever your sector's version of those four titles is, some version of that merger is coming to it, because the borders between the roles were mostly translation work, and translation is the first thing the model absorbed.

## So how fucked are you?

Less than you fear, sooner than you think. The honest reading of all four charts is that the transition is faster than your gut says and less binary than the doom scenario says. Horses did not disappear in 1916; the work shifted for forty years, and the people who drove the carts learned to drive the trucks. The difference between you and the horse is the part that should give you some rest: the horse could not learn to drive. You can.

What disappears first is not your job but the tasks inside it that a model does in seconds: the first draft, the summary, the boilerplate, the routine translation between formats. What appears in their place is the work of directing, checking and owning the result, because a model produces output and you produce judgement. The people in trouble in 2027 are the ones whose entire role was the first draft, and the organisations in trouble are the ones that spent these years scheduling evaluations of whether to begin.

The entry-level question deserves its own honesty, because the scariest prediction came from inside the industry. Anthropic's CEO Dario Amodei [told Axios in May 2025 that AI could wipe out half of all entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years](https://www.axios.com/2025/05/28/ai-jobs-white-collar-unemployment-anthropic), pushing unemployment toward 10 to 20 percent, and he has [doubled down since](https://www.forbes.com/sites/kolawolesamueladebayo/2026/02/21/dario-amodei-doubled-down-on-his-ai-jobs-warning-heres-whats-different-now/). Now hold that against a [fun observation making the rounds](https://x.com/Hesamation/status/2086562765959844121): staffing data shows Anthropic's own zero-to-two-years cohort is currently its largest bucket, bigger than OpenAI's, nearly the size of its ten-to-twelve-years group. No contradiction, just information: the company that expects entry-level work to shrink keeps buying entry-level people, because what evaporates is entry-level tasks, while junior people remain the cheapest way an organisation grows judgement. The juniors who thrive will apprentice in directing models instead of apprenticing in the work models already do.

A Flemish investor recently posted [the corporate to-do list in three bullets](https://x.com/nonkelpier/status/2086379479744627151): reassess every skillset, redesign every process, re-evaluate every system, borrowing Musk's rule that the best process is the one that should not exist, and expecting the back office to feel it first, administration and bookkeeping leading. He is right about the direction, and I would add one calmer note: you do not have to do it all at once. You have to start.

So begin. Take one recurring task you did this week, run it through a model, and honestly score the result. Learn what it does well and where it lies to you, because both lessons pay. And keep your judgement sharp, since that is the seat that survives. If you want a concrete on-ramp, our [Hermiq skills tutorials](/academy/hermiq-skills-tutorial-1-skills-for-your-agents) walk through pointing a model at your own work, on your own hardware. Start today. Step by step. The horse fair of 1915 was not the moment to breed more horses.
